How biographies frame scientists

We looked at 180 English Wikipedia biographies of women and men scientists. Not to judge the scientists themselves, but to look at the words placed around them.

1 Dataset

The collection is small on purpose.

The dataset is a balanced wall of biographies: 18 women and 18 men in each of five STEM fields. Biology, chemistry, computer science, mathematics, and physics are not all of science, but they give us a focused comparison.

The balancing matters. Without it, we might accidentally compare one gender in one field against another gender in another field and call that a gender difference.

The number of biographies is balanced. The raw amount of text is not. Therefore, later comparisons utilize rates in lieu of raw counts.

2 Text

The clearest difference was not family language.

We counted two simple groups of words: science words and family words. The family words were interesting, but they did not survive the strict correction (Bonferroni correction). The science words did.

In this sample, women scientists' biographies used more science-related words per 1,000 cleaned words. That result still held after 1,000 within-field permutations and Bonferroni correction.

Note

The result does not mean women scientists are "more scientific". It means that, in this balanced set of pages, their Wikipedia biographies used science-related vocabulary more densely.

That surprised us, because we expected the strongest difference to be family language. The data did not really support that as a robust claim.

These word and bigram plots are useful for noticing texture, but they can also pick up on confounders, such as individual people, institutions, and places. This is simply the first stage of the analysis, and is for elucidating the data rather than evidence of any patterns.

3 Network

The biographies also make word constellations.

Here the text becomes a map. Each dot is a common word. A line appears when two words show up in the same paragraph. Stronger lines mean the pair appears together more often.

The maps are beautiful, but they are for illustrative purposes. They help show how messy lexical analysis can be. Our findings showed that women was commonly used in womens articles -- we believe that this is due to the women being particularly historically barred from science and academia.

How to read the map: bigger, yellower dots are more connected words. Lines show paragraph-level co-occurrence.

4 Claim

So what can we actually say?

We shuffled gender labels within each field 1,000 times. This gives a simple question: would a difference this large appear if the gender labels were random inside the matched fields?

After correcting for six tested outcomes, only one result stays clearly below the strict threshold: science words per 1,000 cleaned words.

Category Measure Women - men p-value Bonferroni

The cautious conclusion: these Wikipedia biographies do not show a simple story where men get science and women get family. In this sample, the strongest robust pattern is actually more dense science language in women scientists' biographies.

This is still exploratory. It describes Wikipedia framing patterns in this dataset, and not as a statement about the causal nature.
